## 1. HarnessOpt-Bench: Evaluating LLMs at Harness Optimization (arXiv 2608.06301)

**Core mechanism (faithful):** A benchmark, not an architecture. Defines *harness optimization* as: an optimizer (an LLM + coding harness) receives a target agent's seed harness `H₀`, graded dev/validation feedback, and a fixed evaluation budget; it edits the harness as an arbitrary codebase (no semantic partition imposed between prompts, tools, memory, control flow) and nominates a final candidate `H⁺`, scored by normalized gain over the seed on a **held-out test partition inaccessible throughout search**. A trusted execution environment (building on prior work "VERO") enforces this: it blocks access to held-out state, meters per-scope model-call budgets via a gateway, runs every candidate in isolated unprivileged sandboxes, and **versions every candidate for audit**. 5 frontier optimizer models × 4 downstream tasks (fixed-corpus research, terminal use, GAIA, OfficeQA) × both a shared coding harness and each model's native harness = 111 scored runs.

**Evidence quality:** Strong for what it measures. Rigorous protocol (pinned seeds, disjoint dev/val/test splits, disclosure policy, held-out test evaluated only once), 111 scored runs, statistically-aware analysis (resolution bands, Spearman correlations per task), and an honest limitations section (hack-resistant not hackproof; seed-complexity confound not systematically varied; Python-only). Single-lab (Scale AI), first of its kind — not yet independently replicated.

**What QMA should borrow:** The **trusted execution boundary as infrastructure separable from the optimizable content** — sandbox isolation, per-scope budget/allow-list enforcement at a gateway, and mandatory candidate versioning for audit — maps closely onto QMA's provider-unbiased sandbox abstraction and is a good template for what belongs at the kernel/infra layer regardless of what a plugin or harness does inside it. Also useful as an evaluation *method* QMA could adopt for judging its own kernel/harness changes: held-out test partition + normalized gain over seed, rather than trusting visible validation scores (the paper shows visible-best-score is systematically optimistic vs. held-out).

**What to reject and why:** Don't read this as an architecture recommendation — it deliberately imposes **no semantic partition** between prompts/tools/memory/control-flow inside the harness being optimized ("the optimizer may edit, add, or delete files"), i.e. it treats the harness as one undifferentiated codebase. That's a methodological choice for benchmarking, not evidence that a Cordis-style plugin/kernel decomposition is either necessary or unnecessary — the paper is silent on internal harness architecture.

**Kernel implications — mild pressure toward simplicity:** The most load-bearing empirical result is that **optimizer model choice moves gain ~1.8× more than coding-harness choice** (0.142 vs 0.079 average gain-swing), and native (model-specific) harnesses show **no consistent advantage** over a shared harness (11 wins vs 9 of 20 pairs). Read plainly: for the task of *improving an agent*, what surrounds the model matters measurably less than which model is doing the surrounding-editing. This doesn't demand a kernel hook — it's a data point that QMA should not over-invest kernel complexity on the theory that harness sophistication is the dominant lever; model capability dominates, and harness effects are real but secondary and non-monotonic (Fig. 5: harness ranking doesn't hold across models). One caveat worth keeping: on the one task with a genuinely varied harness axis (GAIA), the *native* harness advantage was large but concentrated in specific model-harness pairs — so "harness barely matters" is an average-case finding, not a uniform one.

## 2. From Agent Traces to Trust: A Survey of Evidence Tracing and Execution Provenance in LLM Agents (arXiv 2606.04990)

**Core mechanism (faithful):** A systematic survey building a taxonomy across five dimensions: **trace sources** (reasoning, retrieval, tool-use, memory, environment, multi-agent communication), **evidence/execution units** (documents/observations/tool-outputs/memory-items/claims vs. reasoning-steps/tool-invocations/actions/messages), **provenance relations** (SUPPORT, CONTRADICT, DEPEND-ON, UPDATE, INVALIDATE, drawn from W3C PROV-DM), **recording granularity/timing**, and **trust functions**. Surveys concrete mechanisms per area: provenance-bearing memory (writes need source/lineage metadata: source type, timestamp, authoring agent, supporting evidence, transformation op, confidence, update history), tool-use provenance/information-flow control (CaMeL separates control-flow from data-flow; FIDES does agent-level info-flow labeling; NeuroTaint/Agent-Sentry do semantic taint tracking on tool arguments), and runtime guardrails classified as pre-execution (block a proposed call), in-execution (monitor state), or post-execution (audit/recover).

**Evidence quality:** Secondary evidence — a survey, not new experiments. Its value is in the taxonomy and in identifying the field's central unsolved problem, not in any empirical claim of its own. Well-grounded in established provenance standards (W3C PROV-DM, OpenTelemetry) and a wide primary-literature review (ReAct, Reflexion, ToolLLM, AgentDojo, TRAIL, MAST, CaMeL, FIDES, etc.), but treat every specific system claim inside it as one step removed from the source.

**What QMA should borrow:** The taxonomy itself as shared vocabulary, and — most importantly — the survey's own diagnosis of the field's central open problem (§8.1): **"Few provide interoperable schemas that jointly represent reasoning steps, retrieval, tool calls, tool outputs, memory operations, inter-agent messages, intermediate claims, final answers, and external state changes."** Existing standards (PROV-DM, OpenTelemetry, OpenLineage) are infrastructure-level but not agent-semantic; agent-specific tools (AgentOps, AgentTrace, PROV-AGENT) are closer but still fragmented across frameworks. The paper's own recommended fix is a **unified schema** with canonical entity types (claims, evidence units, tool observations, memory items, environment states, agent messages) and canonical relations (SUPPORT/CONTRADICT/DEPEND-ON/UPDATE/INVALIDATE) that every subsystem writes into.

**What to reject and why:** Don't treat any single cited mechanism (CaMeL, FIDES, NeuroTaint, Agent-Sentry) as validated-for-QMA — they're each one paper's proposal, surveyed at a paragraph's depth, not evaluated head-to-head here.

**Kernel implications — this is the strongest direct evidence in the cluster for a kernel constant, not a plugin.** The survey's central finding is that provenance fragmentation *is* the trust problem: when every tool, memory system, and guardrail defines its own ad hoc logging, nothing can reason across the whole execution to catch e.g. "this tool argument traces back to untrusted retrieved content" (the exact CaMeL/FIDES/NeuroTaint use case) or "this memory item was written from now-invalidated evidence." If QMA implements provenance as a per-plugin, opt-in concern, it reproduces exactly the fragmentation this paper identifies as the industry-wide blocker to trustworthy agents. **Recommendation: the kernel should own one canonical trace/provenance schema and require every subsystem (tools, memory, retrieval, multi-agent messaging) to emit into it — this is a case where "scoped constants" should include a mandatory provenance-emission contract, not a plugin-optional one.** The survey also explicitly separates *pre-execution* guardrails (block before the call) from *post-execution* verification (audit after) — both need the same underlying trace to function, which is further evidence the trace itself is infrastructure, while the guardrail *policy* that reads it can remain pluggable.

## 3. JANUS: Foreseeing Latent Risk for Long-Horizon Agent Safety (arXiv 2607.19913)

**Core mechanism (faithful):** Trains a **predictive guard model** ("Vanguard") that intervenes *before* an unsafe action executes, rather than judging actions already taken. Two coupled tasks trained jointly via a custom RL method (**CoAA-RL**, group-relative policy optimization): an **anticipation** task that, given a partial trajectory `(x, τ_pre)`, samples natural-language summaries of the likely future continuation (not full simulation — abstracted forecasts of safety-relevant events: high-risk tool calls, injection-following, scope expansion); and an **adjudication** task that judges current safety conditioned on both the observed prefix *and* the anticipated future summary. Reward for anticipation blends fidelity-to-true-future with downstream adjudication utility, so the model learns to forecast futures that are actually useful for the safety call, not just plausible ones. Training data comes from multi-agent simulation covering a three-way risk taxonomy: user-originated (disguised harmful intent), environment-originated (indirect prompt injection, tool-output/memory poisoning), and agent-originated (unsafe actions from ambiguous instructions, no malicious intent required).

**Evidence quality:** Solid empirical paper. 4 agent-safety benchmarks (AgentDojo, Agent-SafetyBench, AgentLAB, LPS-Bench), +15.9pp average protection rate over 6 guard baselines (including step-level guards like Llama-Guard-3 and guard *frameworks* like LlamaFirewall/Sandwich Defense), +5.1pp benign-task completion over the guard-baseline average (i.e., it isn't winning safety by over-blocking), and real ablations isolating each design choice: removing anticipation entirely raises average ASR from 0.072→0.148; removing only the inferred future-summary at inference (keeping training-time anticipation) still raises it to 0.123; oracle (ground-truth) future summaries push ASR down to 0.052, bounding the achievable headroom. Its own stated limitation is real and material: training trajectories are simulated, not collected from deployed systems, so generalization to unseen tools/real adversarial adaptation is untested.

**What QMA should borrow:** The **pre-action interception pattern**: the guard consumes the trajectory prefix and a forecast of consequences, and blocks execution *before* the tool call fires, not after. The paper's own framing (Fig. 1) makes the operational point sharply: a reactive guard "detects risk when the unsafe action occurs, too late to prevent harm." For QMA, the transferable design is the **hook shape**, not the Vanguard model itself: a checkpoint that receives (trajectory-so-far, proposed next action) and can veto before execution, with the judgment logic itself swappable.

**What to reject and why:** Don't adopt Vanguard as a component — it's a research artifact (specific model checkpoint, specific training data, simulated trajectories) not production-hardened safety infrastructure. The three-way risk taxonomy (user/environment/agent-originated) is useful conceptually but was authored for benchmark construction, not validated as exhaustive.

**Kernel implications:** Demands a **kernel-level pre-action hook contract** — every proposed tool call/action must pass through an interception point that can (a) see enough trajectory context to reason about delayed consequences, and (b) block execution before it happens. This is a hard requirement on the *shape* of the kernel's execution loop (there must be a synchronous decision point between "action proposed" and "action executed" that a guard can occupy), not optional plugin behavior — a plugin can't retroactively insert a checkpoint the kernel's execute loop didn't leave room for. The safety *judgment* itself (which model, which policy, which risk taxonomy) is naturally pluggable and should stay so — different deployments will want different guard strictness/models. So: **hook point = kernel constant; guard policy = plugin.**

## 4. Engineering Trustworthy Agentic AI for Critical Systems (arXiv 2607.18548)

**Core mechanism (faithful):** A broad survey, not a system. Organizes agentic trustworthiness around **five cross-cutting dimensions** — safety/constraint-satisfaction, robustness/reliability, transparency/interpretability, accountability/auditability, privacy/security — mapped onto a **ten-stage agentic workflow**: perception/observation → reasoning → planning → tool use → communication/coordination → memory update → validation → action/execution → monitoring/re-evaluation → audit/logging. Surveys concrete mechanisms and quantitative metrics per dimension, then examines four constraint-bound engineering domains (power systems, autonomous vehicles/robotics/UAVs, HPC, communication networks) for recurring design patterns and shared failure modes. Introduces a **human–AI autonomy spectrum** (fully autonomous → human-on-the-loop → human-in-the-loop → human-in-command) as a shared vocabulary for expressing where an agent's autonomy boundary sits, arguing this placement should be explicit, domain-specific, and possibly dynamic (grows with positive feedback) rather than generic.

**Evidence quality:** Survey-of-surveys territory — grounded in mature, non-AI-specific safety-engineering standards (SOTIF/ISO 21448, UNECE WP.29 R155, EU AI Act) which lends real rigor to the *vocabulary*, but the agentic-AI-specific synthesis across four domains is aspirational/qualitative (identifying "recurring design patterns" and "shared failure modes" from surveyed literature, not from the authors' own experiments). Treat as a checklist and cross-domain corroboration source, not as proof any specific mechanism works.

**What QMA should borrow:** (a) The **ten-stage workflow decomposition** as a checklist for where QMA's kernel needs explicit hook points — notably it names *validation* and *monitoring/re-evaluation* as distinct stages sitting between planning/tool-use and audit, which is a finer decomposition than "plan → act → observe." (b) The **autonomy spectrum** as vocabulary for QMA's "mind" concept — a mind's bounded authority per task/domain could be expressed on this same advisory → human-in-loop → human-on-loop → human-in-command → autonomous scale, rather than each deployment inventing its own permission language. (c) The paper's naming of **"an unverified boundary between reasoning and action"** as a failure mode common to *all four* independently-surveyed critical-infrastructure domains — this cross-validates JANUS's and the provenance survey's shared premise (a hard checkpoint must exist between deciding-to-act and acting) from a completely different literature (industrial/safety-engineering, not LLM-agent papers).

**What to reject and why:** The domain material (power-grid, UAV, HPC, telecom specifics) is not directly relevant to QMA's general-purpose SDK design and shouldn't be mined for QMA-specific requirements — it's there to justify the cross-domain generalization claim, not to prescribe QMA architecture. Also resist over-indexing on "graded certification regimes" (aviation/automotive-style assurance levels) as a near-term goal — the paper itself frames this as where the field *should* eventually go, not something achievable now.

**Kernel implications:** Corroborating rather than novel — reinforces (from an independent, non-LLM-agent literature) the same two conclusions as papers 2 and 3: (1) provenance/audit trails should be built proactively as a standard, not retrofitted after an incident forces it (Open Challenge D, "Provenance Standards: Build Them Before Regulators Do" — explicitly parallels the provenance survey's framing); (2) the reasoning→action boundary needs an explicit, non-bypassable checkpoint, named here as a *cross-domain* failure mode rather than an LLM-specific one. Adds one genuinely new piece: the **autonomy-spectrum vocabulary** for expressing bounded authority, which QMA doesn't get from the other four papers.

## 5. AppLooper: An Agentic Application Engineering Loop for Accountable Release with Virtual-User Feedback (arXiv 2608.14093)

**Core mechanism (faithful):** A multi-role application-engineering loop built on stock Claude Code (no custom kernel/runtime): an **Application Owner** (confirms frozen requirements, gives feedback, holds sole release authority), a **Development Agent** (only role permitted to modify source; role-prompt explicitly forbids destructive git ops, interactive questioning, and contacting users), a **Virtual-User Agent Cohort** (simulated personas with documented provenance and uncertainty boundaries, execute real browser tasks, cannot modify source, must report failure rather than substitute source-reading for experience), an **Owner-Intent Simulation Agent** (read-only, retests *only* requirements/feedback the owner explicitly confirmed, abstains — records unresolved risk — when evidence is insufficient, max two rounds), a **Testing Agent** (read-only gray-box testing: reproduces reported failures, runs regressions, exercises the UI via real browser, reports defects only), and an **Application-Management Orchestration Layer** that owns feedback routing, task dependencies, candidate versioning, and scheduling — explicitly not itself a coder, tester, or release authority. Every finding/test-result/approval is bound to an immutable **candidate version**; when the candidate changes, prior approval automatically becomes invalid, forcing re-inspection rather than allowing stale sign-off to carry forward.

**Evidence quality:** Weak as an empirical claim — this is a systems/design paper demonstrated through a single worked case study (a todo app), with no controlled benchmark, no user study, no quantitative metrics reported anywhere (no numbers on defect-catch rate, false-positive rate, owner time saved, or comparison against a baseline loop). The mechanism design itself is careful and well-motivated (grounded in software-traceability literature, HCI human-oversight literature), but "it works" is asserted via narrative walkthrough, not measured.

**What QMA should borrow:** Two ideas independent of the evaluation weakness. (a) **Role-scoped permission boundaries expressed as plain natural-language contracts plus orchestration-layer enforcement of what gets promoted where** — e.g. the testing agent is read-only by convention (its role prompt forbids source modification) and the orchestration layer only accepts its output as a "finding," never as a code change; no bespoke kernel primitive enforces this beyond ordinary sandboxing/permission scoping. (b) **Version-bound accountability**: binding every piece of evidence, feedback, and approval to an immutable candidate ID, with approval auto-invalidated on candidate change, is a cheap, general pattern for "who approved what, of what, and is that approval still valid" that doesn't require the heavier provenance-graph machinery papers 2 and 4 argue for — it's closer to ordinary release-engineering discipline (semantic versioning + changelog binding) applied to agent output.

**What to reject and why:** Don't treat this as validated accountability *infrastructure* — with no quantitative evaluation, claims like "abstains when evidence is insufficient" or "reports only observed defects" are role-prompt instructions the paper trusts the LLM to follow, not mechanically enforced guarantees. A model that violates its role prompt (writes source from the "read-only" testing-agent role, or the owner-intent-simulator invents unconfirmed requirements) would not be caught by anything described in the system — there's no independent verification that role boundaries actually held, only that the demonstrated run behaved as expected once.

**Kernel implications — the one paper in this cluster that pulls toward a simpler approach, and should be surfaced prominently rather than smoothed over.** AppLooper achieves what reads as genuine accountability (candidate-bound evidence chains, role-separated agents, an inviolable human release gate) using **no bespoke kernel-level provenance primitive at all** — just an off-the-shelf coding agent, natural-language role prompts, and ordinary application-layer state management (an orchestration layer tracking versions and routing findings). This is a real counter-signal to papers 2 and 4's push for a mandatory, kernel-owned provenance schema: if candidate-version binding plus prompt-scoped role boundaries plus a human release gate gets you a workable accountability story for an application-development loop, QMA should ask whether some of what looks like a "provenance kernel requirement" is actually an **orchestration-layer / plugin-level pattern** that doesn't need to be a scoped constant. The honest reconciliation: AppLooper's role boundaries are *convention-enforced* (trust the model to follow its prompt), not *mechanically enforced* (a kernel-level permission check) — which is exactly the gap JANUS and the provenance survey are worried about (an LLM can be induced to violate its instructions; a kernel-level pre-action checkpoint cannot be prompt-injected around in the same way). So AppLooper is best read as evidence that **light orchestration-layer accountability suffices when consequences are recoverable/low-stakes** (a todo app, human always inspects before real release), not as evidence against kernel-level provenance where actions are irreversible or the deployment lacks a human release gate.

## Cluster verdict

**Top borrowings for QMA:**
1. **Trusted execution boundary as kernel infrastructure** (paper 1): sandbox isolation, per-scope budget/allow-list gateway enforcement, mandatory candidate/action versioning for audit — general enough to sit under any plugin content.
2. **Unified provenance schema as a kernel constant** (paper 2, corroborated independently by paper 4's Open Challenge D): canonical entity types (claims, evidence units, tool observations, memory items, environment states, agent messages) and canonical relations (SUPPORT/CONTRADICT/DEPEND-ON/UPDATE/INVALIDATE) that every subsystem must emit into — the field's own diagnosed failure mode is schema fragmentation from treating this as plugin-optional.
3. **Pre-action interception as a hard kernel hook shape** (paper 3, corroborated by paper 4's cross-domain "unverified reasoning→action boundary" failure mode): a synchronous checkpoint between "action proposed" and "action executed" must exist in the kernel's execution loop; the guard/judgment logic behind it stays pluggable.
4. **Autonomy-spectrum vocabulary for bounded authority** (paper 4): advisory → human-in-loop → human-on-loop → human-in-command → autonomous, as a shared scale for expressing what a "mind" is allowed to do per task/domain.
5. **Version-bound accountability as a lightweight fallback pattern** (paper 5): candidate-ID binding of evidence/approval with auto-invalidation on change — worth having as a cheap orchestration-layer mechanism even where full provenance-graph machinery is overkill.

**Direct challenge to our Cordis-leaning kernel direction (surfaced, not smoothed over):** AppLooper (paper 5) is real evidence that a workable accountability story was built with **no kernel-level provenance primitive whatsoever** — just role-scoped prompts, an application-layer orchestration state machine, and a human release gate, on top of an unmodified off-the-shelf coding agent. This directly cuts against treating provenance/accountability as something that *must* live in the kernel: for at least the class of tasks where consequences are recoverable and a human inspects before anything real happens, convention-enforced role boundaries plus version-bound bookkeeping may be sufficient, and a heavier kernel-mandated provenance schema (papers 2 and 4's recommendation) would be over-engineering. The reconciling view — not a smoothing-over — is that this is a **stakes-dependent** question, not a settled one: AppLooper's enforcement is prompt-level (defeatable by a sufficiently adversarial or confused model), while JANUS and the provenance survey are explicitly motivated by cases (irreversible actions, adversarial/injected inputs, no human in the loop) where prompt-level convention is exactly what fails. QMA should treat "does this deployment have a human release gate and recoverable consequences?" as the actual fork in the design — not assume kernel-level provenance is universally required, and not assume AppLooper's lighter pattern generalizes to irreversible-action or no-human-gate deployments.
